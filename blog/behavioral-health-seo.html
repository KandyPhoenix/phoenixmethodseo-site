Behavioral Health SEO for Group Practices and Clinics

A group practice has more ways to be found than a solo therapist. One team page buries them. Here is the autopsy, the page structure that fixes it, and what to change every time a clinician joins or leaves.

Eight clinicians. One “Our Team” page. Eight headshots, eight two-sentence bios, and a phone number at the bottom.

That is one page asked to stand in for eight clinicians. It is also how a practice ends up ranking for its own name and almost nothing else.

A group has more to rank with. You have more specialties, more modalities, more insurance panels, and often more locations than a solo therapist. Each of those is a search somebody types into Google. Behavioral health SEO for a group comes down to two jobs: get that breadth onto pages Google can find, then keep those pages true as people come and go.

This post came out of a check we run on our own site. In Search Console, the first report we open is Performance. We read the Queries list there, looking for searches where Google already shows our site but no page was built to answer them. Searches about behavioral health SEO were on that list, and we had no page for them. So here is ours.

Why a Group Practice Site Plays by Different Rules

A solo therapist’s site has one person to introduce. A group’s site has a roster, and the roster changes.

  • The searcher is matching. Someone looking at a group may want a specific fit: a clinician who sees teens, takes their plan, does EMDR, and has an opening. Make sure your site answers all four on one path.
  • Referral sources can check you too. Primary care offices, school counselors, and case managers can look you up before they send anyone. A wall of headshots doesn’t tell them who treats what.
  • The facts expire. Clinicians join, leave, go part-time, close their caseload, add a telehealth state, drop a panel. A group site needs an edit every time one of those happens.

The Autopsy: How Group Sites Bury Their Own Clinicians

When we open a group practice site for the first time, these are the causes of death we look for.

  • The single team page. Everyone on one URL. One page is a weak match for “trauma therapist,” “couples counseling,” and “child psychologist” all at once.
  • Ghost clinicians. Someone left your practice a year ago. Their bio is still live, still indexed, still pulling searches for their name. Those callers reach your front desk and hear they’re gone.
  • The missing new hire. Your newest clinician has an open caseload and no page, because the page was “going to get done.”
  • A list instead of pages. Anxiety, trauma, ADHD, grief, couples, and eating disorders in one bulleted list, with nothing behind any of them. A bullet point is not a page.
  • Copy-and-paste locations. Two offices, two location pages, same paragraph with the city swapped. When Google finds pages whose primary content is very similar, it clusters them together and chooses one of them as the canonical. That leaves the choice between your two office pages to Google, not to you.
  • One insurance list for everybody. Panels are listed for the practice, but clinicians can be credentialed differently. A patient can pick a clinician, call, and learn that person is out of network.

Your roster is your keyword list. Every specialty, modality, population, panel, and licensed state your clinicians bring is something a person can search for. Give each one a page.

The Plan: Build the Grid

The grid is three kinds of pages, and every one links to the other two.

1. A real page for every clinician

A pop-up does not count. Neither does an anchor on the team page. Each clinician gets their own URL, something like /team/jane-doe-lcsw/. The page lists their license type, the states they’re licensed in, the concerns and populations they work with, the modalities they’re trained in, the insurance panels they’re on, whether they see clients in person, by video, or both, and whether they’re taking new clients right now. Add Person schema to the page and name the practice in it. Google’s structured data documentation says Google uses structured data to understand the content of a page and to gather information about the people and companies included in the markup.

2. A page for every concern and modality you treat

Anxiety, trauma, EMDR, DBT, couples, and teens each get a page that explains what treatment looks like at your practice and names the clinicians who provide it. These are the pages we build first. It is also the work our content SEO service does: writing those pages from what your clinicians actually offer.

3. A page for every location and every telehealth state

Each office gets its own address, hours, and clinicians. Each state you serve by video gets a page naming the clinicians licensed in it. Different facts make different pages, and that is what keeps them from reading as duplicates.

Then wire it together. The EMDR page links to the three clinicians who do EMDR. Each of them links back to EMDR, to their office, and to their telehealth states. A patient can come in through any door and reach a bookable person in two clicks. The wiring has a second use. Google uses links to find new pages to crawl and as a signal when determining the relevancy of pages.

Underneath, add MedicalClinic schema with each location, and keep the practice name, address, and phone identical everywhere they appear.

Hires and Departures: The Upkeep That Decides Rankings

Building the grid is the easy part. Keeping it true is the job.

When a clinician leaves: don’t just delete the page. A deleted page returns a 404, and indexed URLs that return a 4xx status code are removed from the index. Take the clinician off every concern, location, and telehealth page the same day, then redirect their URL to the specialty page that fits their work best. If they had their own Google Business Profile listing at your address, settle what happens to it before their last day.

When a clinician joins: their page goes live in week one, not quarter two. Add them to every concern, office, and state page that fits. Then run the new URL through URL Inspection in Search Console and request indexing.

When anything else changes: a dropped panel, a closed caseload, a new state license. Each one is a website edit, and somebody has to own those edits. An office manager buried in credentialing may not have the time. That gap is what our post on therapist website management is about: who runs the site, and what they need from you to keep it current.

What We Check First in a Group Practice’s Search Console

We open four reports, in this order. Together they show where the grid is leaking.

  1. Pages report, “Not found (404).” We look here for old clinician URLs that were deleted without a redirect. If a departed clinician’s name still shows in your queries, that URL needs a redirect.
  2. Pages report, “Crawled - currently not indexed.” This is where we check for short bios and copy-paste location pages. Search Console Help defines this status as a page that was crawled by Google but not indexed. Our fix is more specific content on each one, not more pages.
  3. Performance, filtered to pages containing /team/. This shows which clinicians get found and for what. Look for the clinicians whose pages show almost nothing.
  4. Queries with a concern plus a place. “Trauma therapist” plus your city. “Teen counseling” plus your suburb. If those land on your homepage instead of a concern page, the concern page is missing or too thin.

None of that needs a paid tool, only someone with access who looks every month. When the list turns up redirects to fix, schema to add, or pages Google won’t index, that is our technical SEO service.

Start this week with a count: how many clinicians are on your team page, how many are seeing clients today, and how many have a page of their own. Those three numbers show where to start. Frequently Asked Questions

What is behavioral health SEO?

It is SEO for practices that provide mental and behavioral health care, such as group practices, counseling centers, psychiatry clinics, and telehealth practices. It covers the clinician, concern, location, and insurance pages people search for, the technical setup that gets them indexed, and the Google Business Profile work behind the map results.

Should every clinician in a group practice have their own page?

Yes. A page with license type, licensed states, specialties, insurance panels, and availability gives Google something specific to rank and gives patients a way to choose. A shared team page asks one URL to cover every clinician.

What should we do with a clinician’s page after they leave?

Do not delete it and leave a 404. Remove the clinician from every specialty, location, and telehealth page, then redirect their URL to the most relevant specialty page.

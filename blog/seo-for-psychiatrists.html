SEO for Psychiatrists: How a Private Practice Gets Found on Google

Your referrals are full. Your website is not doing anything. Here is the autopsy of a typical psychiatric practice site, and the plan for getting it found by the patients who are searching for you.

Your practice page is on page 7. That is a long way to scroll.

Meanwhile, your Psychology Today profile shows up. A directory shows up. A hospital system with a psychiatry department three counties away shows up. Your own site, the one with your name, your credentials, and your intake link, sits somewhere Google has decided is not worth showing.

That is what happens to a practice website that was built once, by someone who is gone now, and has not been touched since. There are no tricks in SEO for psychiatrists. The work is giving Google enough clear, current, trustworthy information that it feels safe sending a patient to you.

This post came out of a check on our own site, the same one we run on a practice site. We opened the Performance report in Google Search Console and read through the queries, looking for searches where Google was already showing our site but we had no page built for the phrase. “SEO for psychiatrists” was one of them, so we wrote this one.

Why Psychiatry Search Is Not Therapy Search

Advice written for therapists does not always fit. Psychiatry searches look different, and your site has to match them.

  • The searcher may already have a referral. A primary care doctor or therapist said “you should see a psychiatrist.” Now they want to know whether you take their insurance and whether you are accepting new patients. Put both on the site.
  • Plan for service searches, not only modality. “Medication management,” “psychiatric evaluation,” “ADHD evaluation for adults,” “psychiatric nurse practitioner near me.” Each is a different page.
  • Telehealth changes the map. A telepsychiatry practice serves the states where its clinicians are licensed. Name those states on the site, so a person searching from one of them can see that you serve it.
  • The stakes are higher. Google has a name for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people: “Your Money or Your Life” topics. For those, Google’s guidance on helpful content is that its systems give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T, short for experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. So show credentials, authorship, and accurate information on every page.

The Autopsy: Where Psychiatric Practice Websites Die

Nothing hides. When we open up a small psychiatry practice site, these are the causes of death we look for:

  • One “Services” page doing the work of six. Medication management, evaluations, ADHD, telepsychiatry, and anything specialized all crammed into one list. One page is a weak match for six different searches.
  • Provider bios with no credentials. A headshot and a paragraph, but no MD, DO, or PMHNP, no board certification, no training, no states of licensure. For a health site, that is a trust gap.
  • A new clinician who is not on the site. You hired a psychiatric nurse practitioner four months ago. The website still lists two providers. Someone searching for an NP will not find them on your site.
  • An insurance list that is wrong. You dropped a panel last year. It is still on the site. A wrong listing can mean a wasted intake call.
  • An “accepting new patients” notice from years ago. Or a waitlist notice nobody took down.
  • No telehealth page. Telepsychiatry is mentioned in a footer line, with no states, no process, and nothing for Google to index.

Psychology Today is not your website. A directory profile is useful, and it also lists you next to other psychiatrists. Your own site is the only place where you control the information, the intake path, and the authority you build. Use the directory, but do not let it be the only thing Google can find.

The Plan: The Pages a Psychiatric Practice Needs

We start with structure. For a small psychiatry practice, that means the right pages rather than a hundred pages, each built for one search:

  1. One page per core service. That covers medication management, psychiatric evaluation, ADHD evaluation and treatment, and any specialized service you genuinely offer. Each page says who it is for, what the first visit looks like, and how to book.
  2. A full bio for every prescriber. List the degree, board certification, training, licensure states, and the populations they work with. Add Person schema with those credentials, and name the practice in it.
  3. An insurance and fees page. List every panel you are actually on, private-pay rates if you publish them, and what superbills look like for out-of-network patients. Then keep it current.
  4. A telepsychiatry page. It names the states, the services available by video, and what a patient needs to get started.
  5. Practice schema. Use MedicalClinic or Physician markup with your address, hours, and services. Google’s documentation describes local business structured data as a way to tell Google about business hours and the departments within a business. The same page says to use the most specific LocalBusiness sub-type possible.

Under all of it, the site has to load fast on a phone, and Google has to be able to crawl it. If the intake form breaks on mobile, nothing else matters. That plumbing is what our technical SEO service exists to fix: crawl errors, page speed, schema, and mobile layout.

Local SEO and Google Business Profile for Psychiatrists

For an in-person practice, we start with the map pack. Your Google Business Profile needs the right primary category, accurate hours, the correct address or service area, and a link to the matching page on your site, not just the homepage. On the category, Google’s guidance is to choose categories that are as specific as possible, but representative of your main business.

Keep your practice name, address, and phone number identical across the profile, the website, and the directories. Google Business Profile Help says businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to show in local results.

Reviews need extra care in psychiatry. Never reply in a way that confirms someone is or was a patient, even to thank them. A short, general response that invites the person to call the office still shows the profile is active. We cover the full framework on our local SEO page.

The Real Problem: Nobody Is Running the Website

This is the part that gets skipped. The fixes above are not hard, but they never stop.

A new clinician joins. A panel changes. You add telehealth in a new state. Your hours shift for the summer. Every one of those is a website update, and someone has to own it. If the office manager is buried in prior authorizations and whoever built the site has moved on, the site slowly drifts out of date.

That is what dead rankings look like. There was no single disaster, only a hundred small updates that never happened. The fix is somebody who owns the site every month, which means content updates, technical checks, Google Business Profile posts, and a steady stream of pages built around what patients search.

What Progress Actually Looks Like

Straight from Google’s documentation: no one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google. What you can control is how clear, current, and complete your site is.

We recommend this order. Fix the technical foundation first, then build the service, bio, insurance, and telehealth pages, and after that keep publishing and keep the site current. Frequently Asked Questions

Is SEO for psychiatrists different from SEO for therapists?

Yes. Psychiatry searches can be service-based (medication management, psychiatric evaluation, ADHD evaluation) and can come after a referral, when the searcher is checking insurance, availability, and prescriber credentials. The site needs separate service pages, detailed prescriber bios, and an accurate insurance page rather than modality pages alone.

Do I still need a website if I have a Psychology Today profile?

Yes. A directory profile helps people find you, but it sits next to other psychiatrists and you do not control the page. Your own website is where you build authority, keep insurance and availability current, and send patients straight to your intake process.

How long does SEO take for a psychiatric practice?

It depends on your market, your site’s starting condition, and how consistently it is maintained. Google says some changes show in hours and others take months. Google also says no one can guarantee a #1 ranking.

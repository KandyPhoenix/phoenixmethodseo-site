5 Tips for Improving Website Rankings That Actually Move the Needle

Ranking higher on Google isn’t about gaming an algorithm. It’s about closing the gap between what your audience searches for and what your site delivers. These five strategies are where we start.

You have a website and you want it to rank higher on Google. The usual advice is to write more content, get more backlinks, and use keywords. That advice isn’t wrong. It’s just incomplete. Improving your website’s search engine rankings requires understanding why Google ranks pages the way it does, and then aligning your site to those reasons.

These are the five strategies we work through, and what to check for each.

1. Know Exactly Who You’re Writing For

Don’t write content for search engines. Write it for the person doing the search, and directly answer what that searcher actually wants.

Start with Google Search Console. It tells you which queries are sending people to your site and which pages they’re clicking on. That data is the foundation of an audience-first content strategy. If you’re getting impressions for a query but no clicks, check whether your title and meta description match what the searcher expects to find. If you’re ranking on page two for a keyword that already sends you traffic, improve the content quality and relevance of that specific page first.

We start with pages already in positions 8–20. Before you build new pages, identify the existing ones that already show for a search and improve them. This is the approach we use on every Phoenix Method SEO plan: find what’s already showing, then build on it.

Conduct market research beyond keyword tools. Review the questions your actual customers ask in consultations, emails, reviews, and sales conversations. That language belongs in your content.

2. Optimize Your Content for the Full Ranking Signal Stack

On-page optimization is more than inserting a keyword into a title tag. We check a cluster of things on every page: keyword relevance, content depth, page structure, readability, internal linking, Core Web Vitals performance, and E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness). Work on all of them, not only one.

The fundamentals every page should have in place:

  • A single, clear H1 that includes your primary keyword and describes exactly what the page covers
  • Meta title and description that match search intent: genuine previews of what the page delivers, not keyword-stuffed strings
  • Mobile-first structure. Google indexes the mobile version of your page. Check that your layout works on a phone
  • Internal links to related pages on your site that help Google understand your content hierarchy and pass authority where it matters
  • Structured data (schema markup) that tells Google exactly what type of business you run, who your team is, and what services you offer

Check what your platform lets you change. Some platforms limit technical SEO controls such as plugins, schema customization, and server-level performance settings. Our breakdown of WordPress vs. Squarespace SEO capabilities compares what each one lets you control.

3. Build Backlinks on Quality, Not Volume

Backlinks are links from other websites pointing to yours. We look at the quality of those links, not the count. A link from a respected industry publication is worth pursuing. A stack of links from low-quality directories is not.

Backlink strategies for small businesses:

  • Guest posts on authoritative industry sites. Write genuinely useful content for publications your target audience already reads, with a link back to your site.
  • Resource link building. Identify pages in your industry that link out to helpful resources. Create the best version of a resource that would fit on those pages, then reach out.
  • Local citations and directory listings. For location-based businesses, keep NAP (name, address, phone) listings consistent across directories like Google Business Profile, Yelp, and industry-specific directories.
  • Digital PR and mentions. Position yourself as a subject matter expert. Respond to journalist queries, publish data-driven studies or surveys, and make yourself a quotable source in your field. These efforts can earn editorial links.

The mistake to avoid: Chasing volume over quality. A long list of low-authority links is not worth a few strong ones, and spammy link profiles can trigger Google penalties. Build links the way you’d build business relationships: selectively, with a focus on value and relevance.

4. Use Social Media as a Support, Not a Substitute

Social media doesn’t directly cause Google rankings to move. What it does is put your content in front of people, who can then visit your site, share it, search for your business by name, or link to it.

We use social media for SEO as content amplification: publish something genuinely useful on your site, then promote it on the channels where your audience is. Each share extends your content’s reach to people who may link to it, mention your brand, or search for you by name. Our post on social media for private practice covers what posting does and does not do for your Google rankings.

Consistency matters more than platform diversity. It’s better to be active and relevant on two platforms than scattered and intermittent on five. Pick the channels where your audience actually is and publish content that earns engagement, not just impressions.

5. Track the Right Metrics and Iterate

SEO without measurement is guesswork. Track performance at the page level, identify what’s moving and what isn’t, and iterate based on data rather than assumptions.

The core tools and what to watch:

  • Google Search Console. Track impressions, clicks, average position, and CTR for every page. This is where you find the keywords you’re almost ranking for, the pages that are losing visibility, and the queries that bring clicks.
  • Google Analytics 4. Understand which traffic sources convert. Organic search traffic that leaves immediately suggests the page did not match what the visitor wanted. Organic traffic that converts suggests the content matches intent.
  • PageSpeed Insights. Check your Core Web Vitals scores (LCP, INP, CLS). Fix any score in the “needs improvement” or “poor” range.
  • Ranking tracker. If you use one, monitor position changes for your target keywords week over week. Rankings fluctuate. Sustained directional movement is what matters.

Track outcomes. Ranking position and impression count are only part of the picture. Also track organic sessions, lead form submissions, calls, and booked appointments. Build your reporting around what the traffic actually does, not just that it arrived.

The Bottom Line

Improving your website’s rankings isn’t a single action. It’s ongoing work, and each part supports the next. Audience-first content creates pages worth ranking. On-page optimization helps Google understand them. High-quality backlinks build authority. Social amplification extends reach. And consistent measurement shows you what to change next.

If your rankings are stuck, you may already be doing plenty, just not the right things in the right order. A targeted SEO audit shows where your site is falling short and what to fix first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most important factor in improving website rankings?

We start with high-quality, relevant content that directly matches search intent: content that answers exactly what the searcher wants, at the right depth, with the right structure. Technical optimization supports that content. It does not replace it.

How long does it take to see improvements in website rankings?

Google says some changes show in hours and others take months. The timeline depends on your site’s starting point, the competitiveness of your target keywords, and how consistently you work on content and backlinks. Be wary of a promised date. Google also says no one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google.

Do I need to hire an SEO expert to improve my website’s ranking?

Not necessarily. It depends on your market and your time. You can follow a solid on-page optimization checklist and publish consistent content yourself. In a competitive market, where other sites are running active SEO programs, the work takes more expertise and more hours. The question isn’t whether you can do SEO yourself. It’s whether you have the time to keep doing it.

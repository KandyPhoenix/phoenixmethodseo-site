5 Tips for Improving Website Rankings That Have Gone Cold

The work of ranking higher on Google is closing the gap between what your audience searches for and what your site delivers. These five strategies are where we start.

Your rankings have gone cold, and you want your website to rank higher on Google. The usual advice is to write more content, get more backlinks, and use keywords. That advice is right as far as it goes. What it skips is the why. Improving your website’s search engine rankings takes understanding why Google ranks pages the way it does, and then lining your site up with those reasons.

These are the five strategies we work through, and what to check for each.

1. Know Exactly Who You’re Writing For

Write for the person doing the search, not for the search engine, and answer what that person wants.

Start with Google Search Console. It tells you which queries are sending people to your site and which pages they’re clicking on. That data is where writing for your audience starts. If a query brings impressions but no clicks, check whether your title and meta description match what the searcher expects to find. If you’re on page two for a keyword that already sends you traffic, improve the content and relevance of that specific page first.

We start with pages already in positions 8–20. Before you build new pages, find the existing ones that already show for a search and improve them. This is the approach we use on every Phoenix Method SEO plan: find what’s already showing, then build on it.

Look past the keyword tools too. Read the questions your customers actually ask in consultations, emails, reviews, and sales conversations. That language belongs in your content.

2. Fix the On-Page Basics Before Anything Else

On-page optimization is more than putting a keyword in a title tag. On every page we check keyword relevance, content depth, page structure, readability, internal linking, Core Web Vitals performance, and E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness). Work on all of them, not only one.

The fundamentals every page should have in place:

  • A single, clear H1 that includes your primary keyword and describes exactly what the page covers
  • Meta title and description that match what the searcher wants: honest previews of what the page delivers, not keyword-stuffed strings
  • Mobile-first structure. Google uses the mobile version of a site’s content for indexing and ranking, so check that your layout works on a phone
  • Internal links to related pages on your site. Google uses them to find new pages to crawl and as a signal when determining the relevancy of pages
  • Structured data (schema markup) that describes what type of business you run, who your team is, and what services you offer. Google uses structured data to understand the content of the page

Check what your platform lets you change. Some platforms limit technical SEO controls such as plugins, schema customization, and server-level performance settings. Our breakdown of WordPress vs. Squarespace SEO capabilities compares what each one lets you control.

3. Build Backlinks on Quality, Not Volume

Backlinks are links from other websites pointing to yours. We look at the quality of those links, not the count. A link from a respected industry publication is worth pursuing. A stack of links from low-quality directories is not.

Backlink strategies for small businesses:

  • Guest posts on authoritative industry sites. Write something useful for publications your audience already reads. Write it for their readers, not for the link.
  • Resource link building. Find pages in your industry that link out to helpful resources. Build the best version of a resource that would fit on those pages, then reach out.
  • Local citations and directory listings. For location-based businesses, keep NAP (name, address, phone) listings consistent across directories like Google Business Profile, Yelp, and industry-specific directories.
  • Digital PR and mentions. Respond to journalist queries, publish data-driven studies or surveys, and make yourself a quotable source in your field. That work can earn editorial links.

The mistake to avoid: chasing volume over quality. We would take a few strong links over a long list of low-authority ones. Google’s spam policies spell it out: creating links primarily for the purpose of manipulating search rankings is link spam, and sites that violate the policies may rank lower in results or not appear in results at all. Build links the way you’d build business relationships, selectively and for value and relevance.

4. Use Social Media as a Support, Not a Substitute

We do not count on social media to move Google rankings. We use it to put your content in front of people, who can then visit your site, share it, search for your business by name, or link to it.

For SEO, we use social media to get your content seen. Publish something useful on your site, then promote it on the channels where your audience is. Each share puts your content in front of people who may link to it, mention your brand, or search for you by name. Our post on social media for private practice covers what posting does and does not do for your Google rankings.

Consistency matters more than being everywhere. Two platforms you keep up with are better than five you post to now and then. Pick the channels where your audience spends time, and publish content people respond to, not content that only gets seen.

5. Track the Right Metrics and Keep Adjusting

SEO without measurement is guesswork. Track performance page by page, see what’s moving and what isn’t, and make the next change based on the data.

The core tools and what to watch:

  • Google Search Console. Track impressions, clicks, average position, and CTR (click-through rate) for every page. This is where you find the keywords you’re almost ranking for, the pages that are losing visibility, and the queries that bring clicks.
  • Google Analytics 4. See which traffic sources convert. Organic search traffic that leaves immediately suggests the page did not match what the visitor wanted. Organic traffic that converts suggests the page matched.
  • PageSpeed Insights. Check your Core Web Vitals scores (LCP, INP, CLS). Fix any score in the “needs improvement” or “poor” range.
  • Ranking tracker. If you use one, watch position changes for your target keywords week over week. Rankings bounce around, so look for movement that holds in one direction.

Track outcomes. Ranking position and impression count are only part of the picture. Also track organic sessions, lead form submissions, calls, and booked appointments. Build your reporting around what the traffic actually does, not just that it arrived.

How the Five Strategies Fit Together

Improving your website’s rankings is ongoing work, and each part supports the next. We write for the audience first, so each page answers a real search. We work through the on-page basics, so each page is clear about what it covers. We pursue links from respected sites in your field, and we use social media to put the content in front of more people. Consistent measurement then shows you what to change next.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most important factor in improving website rankings?

We start with high-quality, relevant content that directly matches search intent: content that answers exactly what the searcher wants, at the right depth, with the right structure. Technical optimization supports that content and does not replace it.

How long does it take to see improvements in website rankings?

Some changes show in hours and others take months. The timeline depends on your site’s starting point, the competitiveness of your target keywords, and how consistently you work on content and backlinks. Be wary of a promised date.

Do I need to hire an SEO expert to improve my website’s ranking?

Not necessarily. It depends on your market and your time. You can follow a solid on-page optimization checklist and publish consistent content yourself. In a competitive market, where other sites are running active SEO programs, the work takes more expertise and more hours. The question is less whether you can do SEO yourself and more whether you have the time to keep doing it.

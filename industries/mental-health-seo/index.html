Why Mental Health Is Different

Therapy Search Is Specific and Serious

Mental health content falls under what Google calls YMYL (Your Money or Your Life) topics. Google says its systems give even more weight to content with strong E-E-A-T (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness) on those topics. So a therapy site has to show clinical authority on the page, and a plan that leaves that out is not enough for a therapy practice. SEO work can also quietly drift into compliance-sensitive territory: tracking pixels on appointment forms, review widgets, analytics setups. We avoid that territory entirely.

On top of YMYL, therapy searches are unusually specific. Beyond "therapist near me" there is "IFS therapy for anxiety," "EMDR for PTSD [city]," "trauma therapist that takes [insurance]," "Aetna therapist in [neighborhood]," "psychiatrist accepting new patients telehealth." Each of those is a distinct query with distinct intent, and we give each its own dedicated page. A single "therapy services" page cannot answer all of them.

And there is a third layer: upkeep. A mental health practice changes often. A clinician joins, a clinician leaves, a panel gets added, a panel gets dropped, telehealth rules change by state. Each change makes part of the website wrong until someone updates it, and a wrong page fails the patient who lands on it. A large part of mental health SEO is not clever keyword work. It is keeping the site true.

What this page is really about: helping a mental health practice show up when the right patient is searching for the right kind of help, with content written carefully for YMYL, a website that stays accurate, and one place that handles all of it.

The Autopsy

Why Practice Websites Go Dead

When we audit a therapy or psychiatry site, these are the findings we check for first. None of them are exotic. They are the symptoms of a website nobody owns.

Nobody Is Running It

The site was built once and has not been touched since. No one on staff has a login they trust. The practice has grown, and the website still describes the practice as it was when the site went up.

The Clinician Pages Are Wrong

A provider who left is still listed. The new psychiatric nurse practitioner is not. The page that answers "who will I actually see" is inaccurate or missing, and it no longer matches your Google Business Profile or your Psychology Today listing.

The Insurance List Is Stale

"We accept Aetna, Cigna, and BCBS," except you dropped Cigna last spring. A wrong insurance line can mean a wasted intake call, and a "therapist that takes [insurance]" search lands on a page that is no longer true.

Psychiatry

SEO for Psychiatrists and Psychiatric Practices

Psychiatry search is not therapy search. The psychiatry searches we build for fall into three groups: medication management, a diagnostic evaluation, and a prescriber who takes the patient's insurance and has openings. Those are commercial, urgent queries. "Psychiatrist near me accepting new patients," "ADHD evaluation adult [city]," "telehealth psychiatrist [state]," "psychiatric nurse practitioner [insurance]." We answer each of them with a specific page, not a generic "Our Services" page.

Read more

Psychiatric practices also carry a heavy E-E-A-T load. Prescribing is high-stakes YMYL. So we put board certification, licensure, and the prescriber's actual name and credentials on the page, along with clinically accurate descriptions of what a medication consultation involves. We replace anonymous "our providers" copy with named, credentialed, specific copy.

  • Service pages per intent: medication management, psychiatric evaluation, ADHD assessment, TMS or other specialty services each get a page, not a paragraph.
  • Prescriber pages that carry authority: name, credentials, board certification, conditions treated, telehealth availability, and whether they are accepting new patients.
  • Insurance and self-pay clarity: a page that says exactly which panels you are on and what a self-pay evaluation costs. It answers the cost question before a patient has to call.
  • Telehealth footprint by state: if your prescribers are licensed in three states, we build a page for each of the three. Without the page, a search for that state has nothing on your site to land on.

Group Practices

Group Practice and Clinic SEO

A group practice has something a solo practice does not: breadth. Six clinicians means six sets of specialties, modalities, populations, and insurance panels, and each one can be a specific page a patient could land on. Listing everyone on one "Our Team" page with a headshot and two sentences leaves those pages unbuilt.

Read more

The group practice playbook is architecture. Every clinician gets a real page with credentials, specialties, and the concerns they treat. Every modality and every concern the practice covers gets its own page. Those pages link to each other and to the clinician who provides them, so the DBT page built for "DBT therapist [city]" points to the two clinicians who run DBT, each of whom shows as accepting new patients. That is a structure a search engine can read and a patient can use.

It is also a structure that breaks when nobody maintains it. A clinician leaves and her page stays up, sending patients to a provider who is gone. A new hire's specialties never get added. This is why we recommend that a group practice have its website managed, not just optimized once. Our guide to behavioral health SEO for group practices and clinics walks through the full page grid and the upkeep checklist.

Telehealth

Telehealth and Online Therapy SEO

Telehealth changed the geography of a mental health practice. A practice in one city can serve a whole state, and in some cases several states, but the website has to say so. A single footer line saying "telehealth available" gives a state-wide search nothing to land on. So we build a dedicated page for "online therapy in [state]" with the licensure, the technology, the insurance situation, and the clinicians who offer it.

Read more
  • One telehealth page per licensed state: "online therapy [state]," "telehealth psychiatrist [state]," "virtual counseling [state]." Each is a separate search, so each gets its own page.
  • Licensure stated plainly: which clinicians are licensed where. It shows expertise, and it tells a patient right away whether you can see them.
  • Local SEO still matters: if you also see patients in person, we still set up the Google Business Profile and still build for "near me" searches in your home market. Telehealth adds reach; it does not replace local.
  • Platform and privacy honesty: the page names the video platform, says what a first session looks like, and stays free of tracking that does not belong on healthcare forms.

Frequently Asked

Mental Health SEO Questions

How does SEO for therapists differ from regular SEO?

Therapy practice SEO sits at the intersection of YMYL healthcare content and hyper-local search. The searches we build for are by modality (IFS, CBT, EMDR), by specific concern (anxiety, trauma, OCD), by insurance status, and by location, often all at once. Content must be clinically accurate, written carefully for YMYL, and targeted at the specific patient searching for a specific kind of help.

Who should manage a therapy practice's website?

Someone whose job it is. When it is nobody's job, the site goes stale. The options are a staff member with the time and the web skills, or an outside provider who runs the site, the Google Business Profile posts, and the content as one job. Phoenix Method is the second option: keeping the site current is the job, and it is what we do.

How should patient testimonials be handled on my site?

Testimonial handling in healthcare is a regulatory matter, not an SEO one. On our side, we default to external review platforms (Google, Psychology Today, TherapyDen) and don't advise on on-site testimonial decisions.

Should I rank for my specific modality (IFS, CBT, EMDR, etc.)?

Yes. Modality-specific searches are specific about the kind of help wanted. "IFS therapy [city]," "EMDR for trauma," "DBT for BPD" are queries that a dedicated page with clinically accurate content answers better than a general "therapist near me" page can. Your specializations are your differentiation.

Do I need separate content for sliding-scale or private-pay?

Yes. "Sliding scale therapist [city]," "Medicaid therapist," "private pay therapy," and "therapist that takes [insurance]" are all distinct searches. A clear page on fees, insurance, and sliding scale gives each of them an answer and tells a patient up front whether you are a fit.

Is psychiatry SEO different from therapy SEO?

Yes. Psychiatry queries are about medication management, evaluations, and "accepting new patients," and prescribing is high-stakes YMYL. So we put prescriber pages with real credentials and a service page per intent ahead of blog volume. See our psychiatrist SEO guide.

How do I handle online reviews compliantly?

How a practice handles reviews is a question for you and your licensing board, not an SEO one, and we don't advise on it. On our side, we keep review activity on third-party platforms such as Google, Psychology Today, and TherapyDen, and we don't put patient-written content on your site. On-site patient-written content crosses into regulatory territory outside our scope.

What about telehealth? Does SEO work differently?

Slightly. A telehealth practice can serve a broader geographic area (state-wide rather than city-specific), which changes the keyword strategy. State licensure shapes which geographies you can target. We'll map your telehealth footprint against searchable intent and build one page per licensed state.

Can you handle our social media too?

Yes, as an add-on to a monthly plan. LinkedIn and Instagram posts are written from the practice's own pages and posts, scheduled, and reviewed by you before they go up. Social does not rank your website on its own, but it keeps the practice visible and gives your content a second audience.

How long until my phone starts ringing?

Therapy SEO timelines vary by market, competition, and where your site starts. Google says some changes show in hours and others take months. We start with the local work and the Google Business Profile setup, then build the modality-specific pages, then go after competitive YMYL queries (e.g., "anxiety therapy [major city]"), which is the longest part of the job. We do not promise a date or a number of calls.

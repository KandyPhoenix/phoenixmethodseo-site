AI Overviews and Your Rankings: What Google Asks For, and What It Doesn't

A page Google has not indexed is dead to AI Overviews. This guide covers what AI Overviews are, what Google says a page needs to be eligible, and the steps we recommend.

AI Overviews are part of Google Search today. By Google's own account, they began rolling out to everyone in the U.S. on May 14, 2024. We recommend folding them into the SEO you already do instead of running them as a separate project. Where a step in this guide is our own practice and Google does not require it, we label it.

What Are Google AI Overviews, and How Do They Work?

Google AI Overviews are AI-generated summaries that Google shows in its search results for some searches. Google describes an AI Overview as an AI-generated snapshot with key information and links to dig deeper, shown when its systems determine that generative AI can be especially helpful.

They started out under another name. The feature began, Google says, with an experiment in Search Labs called SGE (Search Generative Experience). By its own count, Google answered billions of queries as part of that experiment before launching the revamped experience as AI Overviews in May 2024.

Google's documentation lays out how one is put together. For an AI Overview on a web search, its systems first check that the ranked results are relevant to the query, then give snippets from those pages to the model as the basis for the overview, and then cite and link to the pages that support the information in the response. The model may also run related searches, which Google calls query fan-out, to find pages that cover different parts of the question.

Now for what your page needs. To be eligible to be shown as a supporting link in an AI Overview, Google says a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet. Its documentation adds that the site must also be included in Search generative AI features in Search Console, and that inclusion is the default setting for all properties.

Indexing comes first. Only pages that Google has indexed can be shown as supporting links, so we check indexation, crawl access, and technical SEO health before we do any AI Overview work on a page.

The list stops there. Google's documentation states that there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews, and no other special optimizations are necessary.

What Google Says a Page Needs

  • The page is indexed
  • The page is eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet
  • The site is included in Search generative AI features in Search Console, which is the default

What Google Says Is Not Required

  • Special optimization for AI Overviews
  • New machine readable files, AI text files, or markup
  • Special schema.org structured data

Do not expect one on every search. Google explains that AI Overviews were designed to show up on queries where they add benefit beyond what people already get on Search, and that they often don't trigger.

What Google Says About Clicks From AI Overviews

The fair question is whether AI Overviews cost you clicks. You will not find a click-through percentage in this guide, because we could not confirm one on a Google page.

Google's position is that when people click from search results pages with AI Overviews, those clicks are higher quality, meaning users are more likely to spend more time on the site. It also says AI Overviews give people a jumping off point to explore links, and that with AI Overviews people have been visiting a greater diversity of websites for help with more complex questions.

Your own numbers are the better test. Search Console has a Generative AI performance report that, according to Google, shows how many times links to your site were shown in generative AI features on Google Search, AI Overviews included. Read it next to the regular Performance report before you draw any conclusion about your site.

None of this replaces traditional SEO. Google's own guidance is that the best practices for SEO continue to be relevant, because its generative AI features are rooted in its core Search ranking and quality systems. We treat a supporting link in an AI Overview as one more place a well-built page can appear.

What We Check Before a Page Can Be an AI Overview Source

Google does not require specific optimization for AI Overviews, but its documentation says existing SEO fundamentals continue to be worthwhile. The six checks below are our practice. None is a Google rule, and none is a promise that a page will be cited.

1. Strong E-E-A-T Signals

E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. Google's documentation says E-E-A-T itself isn't a specific ranking factor, and that of these aspects trust is the most important. We start here, checking that the page shows first-hand experience with the topic.

On the page, that means named authors with credentials a reader can verify, original data or first-hand professional experience, citations to authoritative sources, and basics such as SSL and clear contact information. Google's advice is to consider ways to help readers trust your content, such as clear sourcing and background about the author or your site.

2. Direct, Concise Answers in the First 40–60 Words of Each Section

This check is ours, and Google does not ask for it. Its documentation says you don't need to write in a specific way just for generative AI search, and that there is no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI. We put the answer first because it helps the person reading. Answer the implicit question within the first 40–60 words of each heading section.

Don't bury the answer under three paragraphs of preamble. Lead with it, then back it up.

The template we use: [Direct answer in one or two sentences.] [Supporting context: why, how, when.] [Specific example or data point.]

3. Clear Heading Hierarchy (H1 → H2 → H3)

Headings come next. We look for one H1, H2 sections in a logical order, and H3 sub-topics beneath them, so each section covers one distinct, answerable topic. A page with no sub-headings, or one that jumps from H1 to H4, is harder to follow. Google's documentation notes that people generally appreciate pages that are organized by paragraphs and sections, with headings that give a clear structure to navigate.

4. Structured Data That Matches the Visible Page

Google is direct on this one: there is no special schema.org structured data that you need to add to appear in AI Overviews, and you don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, or markup. So we do not add schema to try to get a page cited.

What Google does list among its SEO best practices is making sure your structured data matches the visible text on the page, and that is the check we run. Where a page has a real question-and-answer section, we may mark it up with FAQPage schema that matches the visible text word for word.

Google's documentation says structured data is still a good idea as part of your overall SEO strategy, because it helps with being eligible for rich results. One of those rich results is gone, though. Google now lists the FAQ rich result feature as no longer shown in Google Search results, so we do not add FAQ markup in the hope of a larger listing.

5. Topical Authority Across the Whole Site

We look at how well the whole site covers a topic. That is the case for content clusters: a pillar page on a broad topic, supported by in-depth articles on related sub-topics, all linked to each other. There is a limit, and Google states it. Its documentation says a high quantity of pages doesn't make a website higher quality, and that creating separate content for every variation of a search, primarily to manipulate rankings or generative AI responses, violates its scaled content abuse spam policy.

6. Technical Health: Indexation, Speed, and Mobile

This is the indexing check from the top of the guide, plus the rest of the plumbing. Among the SEO fundamentals that Google's documentation says remain worthwhile are allowing crawling in robots.txt and by any CDN or hosting infrastructure, providing a great page experience, and making sure important content is available in textual form. We also check Core Web Vitals, mobile responsiveness, and clean URL structures. For loading speed, the target in Google's documentation is Largest Contentful Paint within the first 2.5 seconds of the page starting to load. None of this earns a citation on its own, but it comes first.

The 7-Step AI Overview Optimization Action Plan

This is the order we work in. Step one covers what Google requires. The other six are our practice for search as a whole, and none of them promises a citation.

Audit Your Indexation

Open the Indexing report in Google Search Console and find any important pages with noindex errors or crawl blocks. Fix those before any content work. While you are there, open Settings and confirm the Search generative AI control is set to include your site.

Identify Your Informational Query Targets

In Search Console's Performance report, pick out the question-style queries your pages already rank for: "how to," "what is," "why does," "best way to." Search each one in Google and note whether an AI Overview shows and whether your page is one of the supporting links. Some searches will show none. The pages behind those queries are your target pages.

Restructure Content to Lead with Answers

On each target page, rewrite the first 40–60 words after every H2 and H3 so they directly answer what the heading implies, then support the answer. This step is for the reader.

Check Your Structured Data Against the Page

Compare each target page's structured data with what a visitor can see. Google's documentation says not to mark up content that is not visible to readers of the page. Every question and answer in FAQPage markup should match the visible text word for word, and so should names and dates in any other markup.

Strengthen E-E-A-T Signals Across Your Site

Give every key page a named author with credentials and an About the Author section, linked to the author's professional profiles where relevant. For YMYL ("Your Money or Your Life") topics such as health, finance, and legal, include license numbers, certifications, and institutional affiliations.

Build or Expand Your Content Clusters

For each core service or topic, build a thorough pillar page, then add supporting articles on related sub-questions only where each one gives the reader something new. Link them to each other on purpose.

Monitor AI Overview Appearances and Iterate

The Generative AI performance report in Search Console, Google says, shows how your site performs in generative AI features on Google Search, including which pages get the highest and lowest impressions. Check it on a regular schedule, and keep the pages that appear as supporting links accurate and current.

Three AI Overview Optimization Mistakes to Bury

These three tactics sound logical. We recommend skipping all of them.

  • Keyword stuffing answers: Forcing a keyword into every sentence makes the text hard to read. Write the answer for a person first.
  • Adding schema to chase a citation: By Google's own documentation, there is no special schema.org structured data that you need to add for AI Overviews. If you add a FAQ section, write answers that are useful on their own.
  • Using nosnippet to "protect" content: The nosnippet rule applies to AI Overviews, Google's documentation says, and also prevents the content from being used as a direct input for them. If you want a page to stay eligible as a supporting link, leave it off.

Frequently Asked Questions About AI Overviews

What are Google AI Overviews?

Google AI Overviews are AI-generated summaries that Google shows in its search results for some searches. Google says an AI Overview gives an AI-generated snapshot with key information and links to dig deeper, and that it appears when Google's systems determine that generative AI can be especially helpful.

How do I get my content cited in Google AI Overviews?

No one can promise a citation, and we don't. Google says a page must be indexed, and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet, to appear as a supporting link in an AI Overview. The site must also be included in Search generative AI features in Search Console, which is the default setting. Beyond that, Google's documentation says there are no additional requirements and no other special optimizations are necessary. Do AI Overviews hurt organic click-through rates?

We do not quote a click-through figure, because we could not confirm one on a Google page. What content format works best for AI Overview visibility?

Google does not ask for a special format. Its documentation says you don't need to write in a specific way just for generative AI search, and that there is no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces. For the reader's sake, we put the main answer within the first 40–60 words of each heading section and use lists where they make the page easier to follow.

Can I opt out of appearing in Google AI Overviews?

Yes. Google says the Search generative AI control in Search Console lets you exclude your site's links and content from AI Overviews and AI Mode, and that the control isn't used as a ranking or inclusion signal for other parts of Search. For a single page or passage, Google's documentation points to the nosnippet, data-nosnippet or max-snippet controls. Excluding your site also means it can't be shown as a supporting link, so unless you have a reason to opt out, we recommend staying included.

Does AI Overview optimization conflict with traditional SEO?

No. Google's documentation says that optimizing for generative AI search is still SEO, and that the best practices for SEO continue to be relevant. We do the same core work for both, so treat AI Overviews as one more part of the SEO you already do.
